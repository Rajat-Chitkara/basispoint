Methodology

How a report is built

"Traceable to a source document" is a claim, and claims should be checkable. This page sets out exactly what that means in practice — the order sources are trusted in, how figures are verified against each other, and what is deliberately excluded.

1. The source hierarchy

Every figure is traced to the highest-ranking source available. Where a lower-ranked source is the only one available, the report says so at the point the figure is used.

  1. Audited financial statements — annual accounts and their schedules and notes. The notes are usually where the answer actually is.
  2. Statutory filings — documents companies are legally required to file, including MCA filings and their attachments.
  3. Regulatory disclosures — filings made to a sector regulator, and the regulator's own published aggregates and circulars.
  4. Scheme- or product-level disclosures — where an industry publishes at instrument level, that level is used in preference to any industry total.
  5. Company-published primary material — investor presentations and press releases, used only for context, never as the basis of a headline number.

What is not used

  • Syndicated market-research databases whose own sourcing cannot be inspected.
  • Market-size figures cited in news coverage or industry decks without a traceable origin.
  • Estimates from parties with a commercial interest in the number being large.
  • Any figure that cannot be reconstructed from a document a reader could retrieve.

2. Measuring rather than inheriting

Where an industry total is commonly cited, the total is not taken on trust. It is rebuilt from the bottom by measuring the individual units that compose it and reporting the coverage achieved. If 39% of an industry has been measured directly, the report says 39% — it does not quietly gross the figure up to 100% and present the result as measured.

Where grossing up is unavoidable, the extrapolation is stated separately from the measured base, so a reader can discard it and keep the measurement.

Issue 01 labels this explicitly: figures are measured at scheme and AMC level, directional at industry level. The three-AMC sample is measured; the industry pool implied from it is an extrapolation that assumes the observed relationship between passive share and effective rate holds across the industry. Both are in the report, and they are not presented as the same kind of number.

3. Cross-verification

Every measured aggregate is reconciled against an independent path to the same number — typically a disclosed total from a different document, produced by a different party for a different purpose. The gap between the two is reported, not hidden.

A worked example — Issue 01. Each scheme's investor-education charge was extracted two independent ways from the AMC scheme annual reports. First, from the related-party disclosure of the scheme's contribution to AMFI — since 50% of the levy is transferred by mandate, doubling it recovers the total charge. Second, from the scheme-level investor education and awareness expense reported under other expenses.

For SBI Mutual Fund the two methods produced ₹175.25 crore and ₹174.96 crore — a gap of 0.17%, with 61 of 64 tested schemes agreeing within 1%. Those figures are printed in the report, because a reconciliation you cannot see is not a reconciliation. A gap that size supports the measurement; a gap of 15% would have meant the method was wrong, and the report would have said so instead.

4. Assumptions are visible and separated

Analysis requires assumptions. The rule here is that they are never buried in a calculation. Each report distinguishes three things explicitly:

  • Measured — read directly from a source document. Cited.
  • Derived — calculated from measured figures. The calculation is shown.
  • Assumed — a judgement. Stated as such, with the reasoning, and sensitised so you can see what happens if the assumption is wrong.

Delivered models carry the same discipline: assumptions live in one labelled block, are never hard-coded into formulas, and every output traces back to them.

5. Sensitivity, not false precision

Where a result depends on an uncertain input, the report gives a range and identifies which variables actually move the number. A single-point estimate carried to three decimal places implies a confidence the underlying data does not support.

6. Corrections

If a figure is wrong, the correct response is to say so in public. Errors are published as dated amendments to the report rather than silently edited, so a reader who downloaded an earlier version can tell what changed. Corrections can be sent to contact@thebasispoint.in and are read.

7. What you receive

On commissioned work the same standard applies, and the deliverable includes the working — not just the conclusion:

  • The report or model itself.
  • A sources appendix listing every document used, with enough detail to retrieve each one.
  • The assumptions block, separated as above.
  • A walkthrough call to defend the numbers directly.
